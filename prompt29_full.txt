## 1. Map the actual data lifecycle

Inspect the existing database models, storage services, APIs, background jobs, and frontend workflows.

Inventory the actual data categories, including where applicable:

- Assets and asset observations.
- Assessment jobs and execution history.
- Findings, severity assessments, and remediation status.
- Raw and normalized evidence.
- Evidence hashes and provenance records.
- Knowledge graph nodes, edges, and derived relationships.
- Attack paths and risk calculations.
- Remediation approvals and independent retest results.
- Audit events and security decisions.
- User, authentication, and authorization records.
- Learning datasets, prediction records, and model metadata.
- Logs, exports, temporary files, backups, and recovery artifacts.

For every category, determine:

1. Where it is stored.
2. Whether it contains sensitive or identifying information.
3. Which components depend on it.
4. Whether it is authoritative, derived, or reproducible.
5. Whether it has an established retention policy.
6. Whether it can be archived, redacted, or deleted safely.
7. Whether its deletion affects auditability, graph consistency, risk calculations, or learning.

Do not assume that all stored data is evidence or that all derived data can be safely regenerated. Verify the actual dependency relationships.

## 2. Introduce explicit data classification

Where the current architecture lacks a consistent classification model, implement a practical classification system.

Potential categories include:

- Operational metadata.
- Asset and configuration information.
- Security findings and risk assessments.
- Raw assessment evidence.
- Credentials or secret-like material discovered during assessment.
- Audit and approval records.
- User and authentication data.
- Derived analytics and learning artifacts.

Adapt these categories to the actual application rather than adding an unnecessarily complex enterprise taxonomy.

Define which categories may be displayed, exported, archived, redacted, or deleted by each authorized role.

Requirements:

- Classification decisions must be enforced server-side.
- Frontend visibility must not substitute for authorization.
- Sensitive evidence must not automatically become accessible to every user who can view a finding.
- Data exports must respect the permissions and scope of the requesting user.
- Classification changes must be auditable where they affect security or access decisions.
- Unclassified data must not automatically receive the least restrictive handling.

## 3. Build a configurable retention-policy engine

Inspect whether retention behavior currently exists. Reuse it where practical.

Implement a policy mechanism that can define, for applicable data categories:

- Retention duration.
- Archive eligibility.
- Deletion eligibility.
- Required approval.
- Legal or investigation hold behavior, if supported by the product requirements.
- Dependencies that must be preserved.
- Whether a record is eligible for redaction or anonymization.
- The expected handling of expired records that cannot yet be safely deleted.

Requirements:

1. Use explicit, validated policy configuration.
2. Avoid hardcoding unrelated retention durations throughout application code.
3. Provide safe defaults and clearly documented examples.
4. Treat missing or invalid policy configuration conservatively.
5. Preview affected records before executing destructive operations.
6. Record policy decisions and execution outcomes.
7. Make policy execution idempotent.
8. Prevent a record from being deleted while a required dependency or hold prevents deletion.
9. Distinguish policy expiration from actual deletion.
10. Do not invent legal retention periods or claim regulatory compliance without a verified basis.

Use the application's existing authorization model. If retention-policy changes require privileged approval, enforce that requirement on the backend.

## 4. Implement a safe retention preview and execution workflow

Create an auditable lifecycle workflow that separates planning from execution.

A preview should report, at minimum:

- Records eligible for action.
- Records excluded from action.
- The policy responsible for each decision.
- Relevant dependency or hold restrictions.
- The estimated scope of the operation.
- Any unresolved integrity or storage errors.

Execution must revalidate the preview's assumptions before changing data.

Requirements:

- Require appropriate authorization for destructive actions.
- Require explicit confirmation or approval when the operation is high impact.
- Prevent stale previews from authorizing changes after relevant policy or record state has changed.
- Use bounded batches for large operations.
- Track progress and partial failures.
- Prevent overlapping executions of the same lifecycle operation.
- Make retries safe and idempotent.
- Preserve a clear audit record of the operator, policy, scope, and outcome.
- Never report successful deletion when some targeted records remain.

Provide a dry-run mode and deterministic tests. Do not execute broad deletion against the current development database as part of implementation.

## 5. Preserve audit integrity while managing retention

The VAPT System needs to preserve trustworthy records of security decisions and lifecycle operations.

Inspect the existing audit chain, event hashing, evidence provenance, and recovery mechanisms.

Implement a coherent retention strategy that distinguishes:

- Data that may be deleted.
- Data that may be redacted.
- Data that must be retained under current policy.
- Data whose integrity metadata must survive removal of its payload.
- Data that cannot be removed until dependent records are reconciled.

If raw evidence is removed, determine whether a minimal audit record can preserve its identifier, original digest, creation time, deletion authorization, policy reference, and deletion outcome without retaining the sensitive payload.

Do not claim that a hash proves the truth of deleted evidence. A digest can help detect changes to known content but cannot independently reconstruct or validate content that is no longer available.

If the existing append-only audit design conflicts with deletion requirements, document and implement a deliberate strategy rather than silently rewriting historical events or breaking the audit chain.

Test that lifecycle operations cannot silently invalidate predecessor hashes, provenance links, or audit verification.

## 6. Design secure evidence redaction and secret handling

Security assessment evidence may accidentally contain passwords, tokens, private keys, session cookies, personal information, or confidential configuration.

Inspect evidence ingestion and display paths.

Where justified, implement:

- Detection of common secret-like patterns.
- Redacted previews for authorized users.
- Explicit separation between original evidence and sanitized representations.
- Restricted access to original evidence.
- Redaction metadata that identifies the transformation without exposing the secret.
- Safe export behavior that does not inadvertently restore redacted material.
- Tests using synthetic secrets.
- Clear warnings when automated detection is incomplete.

Do not modify the original evidence in place merely to make a preview safer if doing so would invalidate its digest or destroy forensic value.

Preserve original evidence when required by the current policy, and restrict access appropriately. If a retention policy permits deleting the original, use the controlled lifecycle process.

Avoid logging matched secret values or copying them into audit events.

Do not claim automated redaction detects every secret format.

## 7. Implement safe export and archival controls

Inspect the existing export, report, backup, and evidence-download functionality.

Implement or strengthen controls so that:

- Exports contain only records the requester is authorized to access.
- Export scopes and filters are validated server-side.
- Large exports are bounded or processed asynchronously.
- Export filenames and archive paths are safe.
- Archive contents are inspected before release.
- Sensitive fields are redacted where required by policy.
- Export metadata identifies generation time, scope, and applicable redaction behavior.
- Temporary export files are cleaned up safely after their permitted lifetime.
- Failed exports do not leave partial files accessible as completed reports.
- Export operations are auditable without copying the entire sensitive payload into logs.

For archival, verify that archived data can be retrieved and checked for integrity when required.

Do not assume that moving a file to an archive automatically makes it secure or that a backup automatically complies with deletion policies.

## 8. Reconcile retention with backups and recovery

Inspect backup creation, restoration, and data-retention behavior together.

Identify what happens when a record is deleted from the live database but still exists in:

- SQLite database snapshots.
- WAL or SHM files.
- Application backups.
- Export archives.
- Temporary storage.
- Historical release artifacts.
- Derived datasets or cached representations.

Implement a documented approach for handling these copies within the actual capabilities of the system.

Requirements:

1. Clearly distinguish deletion from the live database from expiration of every backup copy.
2. Ensure restoration does not silently resurrect records that must remain deleted under the applicable policy.
3. Where necessary, maintain a minimal, access-controlled deletion or suppression record that can be reapplied during restoration.
4. Protect suppression metadata against unauthorized modification.
5. Verify that retention and suppression state is consistent after restoration.
6. Do not destroy the only viable recovery copy before a replacement has been verified.
7. Do not claim immediate or universal deletion when backup copies remain.
8. Do not restore old data over newer records without explicit authorization and verified recovery conditions.

Preserve the existing bounded recovery and integrity-verification controls.

## 9. Protect graph, risk, and learning consistency

Evidence and findings may influence the knowledge graph, contextual risk scores, attack paths, monitoring decisions, and adaptive learning.

Before deleting or redacting a record, identify and handle its downstream dependencies.

Implement appropriate behavior for:

- Findings that reference expiring evidence.
- Graph nodes and edges derived from removed observations.
- Risk calculations that depend on unavailable evidence.
- Attack paths whose supporting relationships become invalid.
- Historical predictions and their recorded outcomes.
- Training examples derived from evidence that is later restricted or deleted.
- Cached or materialized summaries containing removed information.

Requirements:

- Do not silently treat unavailable evidence as proof that a security issue has disappeared.
- Distinguish deleted, unavailable, expired, invalidated, and verified-negative evidence where appropriate.
- Preserve enough provenance to explain prior security decisions when policy permits.
- Recompute derived state when required, or explicitly mark it stale until recomputation completes.
- Prevent restricted or deleted data from silently re-entering future learning datasets.
- Keep historical outcome records consistent with the system's actual retention and governance policy.

Do not delete authoritative findings merely because one supporting evidence item expires. Evaluate whether alternative evidence or independent verification still supports the finding.
